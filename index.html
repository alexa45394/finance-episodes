Project writeup

About this project

This project asks a simple question: if you put $100 into an asset at the start of a market crisis, what actually happens to it? We wanted to test finance labels that people repeat often, like gold is safe, bonds are safe, tech is risky, or Bitcoin is digital gold. Instead of telling viewers what to believe, the visualization lets them compare those labels across the same crises, the same $100 starting point, and the same S&P 500 benchmark.

The story begins with cash because even doing nothing is not neutral. The inflation chart shows that $100 loses purchasing power over time, so every asset has to beat that quiet loss before it can really be called safe. After that, the viewer picks an asset and first sees an information card explaining what it is, whether it belongs to old money or new money, and what happened to that specific asset in the selected episode. This makes the later charts easier to understand because the viewer knows what they are looking at before watching the line move.

The main stress test then follows the selected asset through one crisis as a $100 line chart, with SPY kept on the chart as a benchmark. A ranked bar chart shows how every asset finished in the same episode, so the viewer can see whether their asset actually outperformed or just lost less badly than the market. The next section compares old money and new money directly. The group cards summarize the difference, and the old-versus-new line chart turns that comparison into a visual pattern.

The long-view chart expands the story beyond a single crisis. Viewers can select up to five assets while SPY stays locked on, use log scale for extreme assets like Bitcoin, and follow the same asset through multiple crisis windows. This interaction is important because one crisis is not enough to define an asset. Bonds can help in one environment and struggle in another. Tech can crash during the dot-com period but dominate during the AI rally. The market sheet and clickable notes give extra context for each asset, while the portfolio builder lets viewers test their own mix against the S&P 500.

One design challenge was balancing storytelling with exploration. Too many assets at once would create overplotting, so the long-view chart limits the number of selected assets and uses distinct line colors. The green crisis bands act as annotations that connect the data to historical episodes. The asset notes also handle missing or partial data, such as ETFs that did not exist during earlier crises, so the viewer can understand gaps instead of treating them like errors.

The main takeaway is that there is no universally safe asset and no investment that wins every crisis. Safety depends on what kind of crisis you are in. This visualization succeeds because it lets viewers test that idea directly: they can choose an asset, watch the same $100 move through a crisis, compare it against old money, new money, and SPY, and then see whether that story still holds over time.

Data: historical Yahoo Finance prices via yfinance. The $100 framing is hypothetical — real prices, hypothetical investment scenario. ETF start dates and missing-history notes are included where an asset proxy did not exist for an older episode.
